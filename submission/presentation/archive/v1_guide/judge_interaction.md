# Judge Interaction Guide

How to present yourself, read judges, and handle different situations. This is the non-technical preparation that separates winners from runners-up.

---

## Body Language Basics

- **Stand straight, shoulders open.** Not rigid -- relaxed but upright. No crossing arms.
- **Make eye contact.** When answering, look at the judge, not the poster. Glance at the poster only when pointing to a specific figure.
- **Gesture toward the poster naturally.** Open palm, not pointing finger. Guide their eye to the relevant section. Then come back to eye contact.
- **Stay in your space.** Never step into the aisle. This can literally get you disqualified at CSEF.
- **Hands visible.** Not in pockets. Rest at your sides or use them to gesture. Natural is fine.
- **Smile when greeting.** Genuine, not forced. Then let your expression match the conversation -- serious when discussing disease, engaged when discussing results, thoughtful when discussing limitations.

## Voice & Tone

- **Speak clearly and at a moderate pace.** Not rushed, not slow. You'll naturally speed up under nerves -- consciously slow down by 10%.
- **Vary your pitch.** Monotone kills engagement. Let enthusiasm come through when talking about discoveries. Let weight come through when discussing Alzheimer's impact.
- **Don't upspeak.** Statements are statements, not questions. "The alignment was 72 percent" not "The alignment was 72 percent?"
- **Pause before answering hard questions.** A 2-second pause while you think shows confidence. Blurting out an answer shows nervousness.
- **Use "I" not "we."** This is your individual project. "I discovered" not "we found."

## The First 10 Seconds

When a judge walks up:

1. Make eye contact and smile
2. "Hi, I'm Amaar. Thanks for coming by."
3. Wait for them to initiate. They might say "tell me about your project" or jump straight to a question.
4. If they say "tell me about it" -- use your 60-second opening
5. If they ask a specific question immediately -- answer it. Don't force your prepared speech.

## Five Judge Types (and how to handle each)

### The Warm Judge

Asks open questions, nods a lot, seems genuinely interested. Smiles.

- **Strategy:** Let the conversation flow naturally. This judge wants to like your project. Be genuine, show passion, let them guide the discussion. They'll advocate for you in the caucus if they feel a personal connection.
- **Risk:** Spending too long chatting and not covering key results. Make sure you hit your numbers even in a friendly conversation.

### The Cold Judge

Asks pointed questions, minimal facial feedback, takes notes, poker face.

- **Strategy:** Answer concisely. Don't over-explain or ramble -- they're testing whether you crack under pressure. Short, precise, data-backed answers. If they're silent after your answer, that's OK. Don't fill the silence with unnecessary words.
- **Risk:** Interpreting coldness as hostility. They're probably just focused. Keep your composure.

### The Skeptical Judge

Challenges every claim. "Really? Are you sure? How do you know? Isn't that a stretch?"

- **Strategy:** Never get defensive. Start with "That's a fair point" or "You're right to question that." Then respond with data. They respect intellectual honesty over confident hand-waving. If they find a real weakness, acknowledge it: "That is a limitation, and here's why the work is still valuable despite it."
- **Risk:** Getting flustered or contradicting yourself. Stay calm. If you don't know, say "I haven't tested that specifically."

### The Expert Judge

Knows more about some aspect than you -- maybe a neurologist, maybe a biostatistician.

- **Strategy:** Don't pretend to know more than you do. If they correct you, say "I didn't know that -- thank you. That's useful context." Then connect it to your work. Experts respect students who learn in real time. They'll be your strongest advocates if they see genuine understanding.
- **Risk:** Trying to BS them. They'll know instantly. Honesty always.

### The Confused Judge

Doesn't follow the EEG/ML parts. Asks for clarification. Seems lost.

- **Strategy:** Drop to analogy level immediately. "Think of it like a weather forecast for the brain." Or "It's like a thermostat versus a space heater on a timer." Don't talk down -- simplify. Point to figures and let visuals do the work. Keep your sentences short.
- **Risk:** Losing them entirely and them remembering your project as "the confusing one." Make sure they walk away understanding ONE thing: you predict when the brain needs therapy and deliver it at the right time.

## Handling Interruptions

Judges will interrupt you. This is GOOD. It means they're engaged and want to go deeper on something specific. When interrupted:

1. Stop immediately. Don't try to finish your sentence.
2. Listen to their question.
3. Answer it directly.
4. If their question leads naturally to the next section of your presentation, continue from there.
5. If it's a tangent, answer it, then say "Would you like me to continue with the results, or is there another area you'd like to explore?"

## Handling "I Don't Know"

It will happen. A judge will ask something you genuinely don't know. This is not a disaster.

- **Say:** "I don't know the answer to that specifically." (Never bluff.)
- **Then:** "But based on [related thing you do know], I would hypothesize [educated guess]. That would be a good follow-up experiment."
- **Or:** "That's outside what I tested, but I can tell you [related thing you did test]."

Judges rank a student who says "I don't know but here's how I'd find out" far above a student who makes something up. The handbook explicitly says to watch for students who "improperly use jargon" and "don't understand terminology." Admitting gaps demonstrates the opposite.

## Handling Rude or Dismissive Judges

Rare, but possible. A judge who seems uninterested, checks their phone, or asks dismissive questions.

- **Stay professional.** Answer their questions as if they're the most important judge of the day.
- **Keep answers short.** Don't waste time on someone who isn't listening.
- **Don't take it personally.** They might be tired, have judged 20 projects already, or be having a bad day.
- **End gracefully:** "Thanks for your time. Do you have any other questions?"

Remember: you need multiple judges to advocate for you. One dismissive judge doesn't matter if four others are enthusiastic.

## Ending an Interview

The judge will often signal they're done by stepping back or saying "Thank you." You close with:

- "Thanks for the great questions. Is there anything else you'd like to dig into?"
- Or: "Do you have any questions for me?"

If you want to leave them with one memorable fact:

- "The sentence I'd leave you with is: the system predicted when 35 patients' brains would lose therapeutic response, and it was right 83% of the time, for every single patient."

## Energy Management (4+ hours of judging)

- **Eat before judging starts.** Protein, not sugar. You need sustained energy.
- **Water at your table.** Sip between interviews. Dehydration kills focus.
- **During breaks:** Sit down. Close your eyes for 30 seconds. Reset. Review your numbers sheet.
- **Stay standing during interviews.** Don't lean on the table.
- **If you feel your energy dropping:** Take 3 deep breaths, roll your shoulders, reset your posture. Physical reset triggers mental reset.
- **After a bad interview:** Forget it immediately. The next judge hasn't seen you yet. Every interview is fresh.

## The Caucus Sentence

After judging, your judges go into a room and discuss. They need to remember you and argue for you. Give them ONE sentence they can retell:

**"He built a system that predicts when an Alzheimer's patient's brain is about to lose its response to therapy -- five seconds before it happens -- and it worked on all 35 patients."**

Everything in your presentation should reinforce this sentence. Every answer should circle back to it.
