# Exigence and Motivation

## Personal Connection

My grandmother had dementia. I watched her lose the ability to recognize her own family, to carry a conversation, to remember what she had said moments earlier. That experience left a mark — the feeling of watching someone you love disappear while still physically present. When I began planning a research project for the Synopsys Science and Engineering Fair, I knew I wanted to work on something that mattered beyond a grade. Alzheimer's disease was personal.

I have also played piano for eight years. Music requires constant synchronization between rhythm perception, motor planning, and working memory — processes that depend on precisely the neural oscillations disrupted in Alzheimer's. That intersection between auditory processing and brain rhythms drew me to the science of neural entrainment: the idea that external rhythmic stimulation can drive the brain to oscillate at specific frequencies, restoring function that disease has degraded.

## The Scale of the Problem

Alzheimer's disease affects over 55 million people worldwide. It is the most common cause of dementia, responsible for 60-80% of cases, and its prevalence is projected to triple by 2050 as populations age. There is no cure. Current FDA-approved medications (cholinesterase inhibitors, NMDA receptor antagonists, and the newer anti-amyloid antibodies) provide modest symptomatic relief or slow progression in subsets of patients, but none halt or reverse the underlying neurodegeneration.

The economic burden is staggering — over $300 billion annually in the United States alone — and the human cost immeasurable. Caregivers, often family members, face years of progressive loss. The disease strips away memory, language, reasoning, and eventually the ability to perform basic activities of daily living.

## A New Therapeutic Frontier

In 2016, a landmark study from MIT (Iaccarino et al., Nature) demonstrated something remarkable: exposing Alzheimer's model mice to flickering light at 40 Hz — the gamma frequency — triggered the brain's immune cells (microglia) to clear amyloid-beta plaques by 40-50%. Follow-up work showed that combined audiovisual 40 Hz stimulation promoted clearance through the brain's glymphatic system, essentially "flushing" toxic proteins through cerebrospinal fluid pathways (Nature, 2024). Human clinical trials by Cognito Therapeutics demonstrated slowed brain atrophy and preserved cognitive function in treated patients, with plasma biomarker reductions of 19-47% in responders (Chan et al., 2025, Alzheimer's & Dementia).

This is non-invasive, non-pharmacological, and remarkably simple in concept: sound or light at 40 Hz, delivered daily, can modify disease pathology. It represents one of the most promising intervention strategies in Alzheimer's research.

## The Gap That Motivated This Project

Despite the promise of 40 Hz entrainment, current clinical protocols treat every patient identically: 40 seconds on, 20 seconds off, repeated for an hour. This is a blunt instrument. Research consistently shows that approximately 30% of patients are non-responders — they show minimal entrainment despite identical stimulation. Among responders, individual trajectories diverge: some habituate (declining response over time), others sustain or even increase their coupling.

No existing system adapts to these differences. No system predicts when a patient's brain is about to lose entrainment and intervenes proactively. No system conserves stimulation by resting when the brain is already well-entrained. The gap is between a therapy with genuine disease-modifying potential and a delivery mechanism that ignores the very individual variability that determines its efficacy.

That gap — between the promise of 40 Hz therapy and the crudeness of fixed-schedule delivery — is what this project addresses. The goal was not to discover a new therapy, but to make an existing one substantially better by making it adaptive, predictive, and personalized.
